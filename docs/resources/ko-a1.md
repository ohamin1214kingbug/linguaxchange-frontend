# Korean TOPIK 1 (A1) — What to Study

**Level:** TOPIK 1 · CEFR A1 (Breakthrough) · **Language:** Korean · **Audience:** Learners

Korean has a gate that Spanish and German do not. Before any grammar makes sense you have to be able to read, and until you can, every word you meet is a picture rather than a sound. The good news is that this gate is unusually cheap to pass: Hangul is a designed alphabet, not an inherited one, and most learners read it — slowly, but genuinely — within a week.

TOPIK level 1 describes someone who can carry out basic conversations for daily survival: introducing yourself, buying something, ordering food. You can understand and produce simple sentences about very familiar subjects — yourself, your family, your hobbies, the weather — using roughly 800 basic words and a small set of sentence patterns.

Everything below serves that description. If a grammar point does not help you survive one of those situations, it is not a TOPIK 1 problem yet.

## Before grammar: Hangul

Do not postpone this and do not use romanisation as a crutch. Learners who lean on romanised Korean spend the following year unlearning the pronunciation it taught them.

- The 14 consonants and 10 vowels, and how they assemble into syllable blocks
- The three-way consonant contrast that has no English or Spanish equivalent: plain ㄱ, aspirated ㅋ, tense ㄲ
- Vowel pairs European ears merge at first: ㅓ/ㅗ, ㅡ/ㅜ, ㅐ/ㅔ
- Final consonants (받침) and the seven sounds they collapse into
- The main sound-change rules, because Korean is not written as it is spoken: 좋아요 sounds like 조아요, 한국말 like 한궁말

Give this two weeks of daily practice. It pays back for the rest of your study.

## Grammar to master

**Word order and particles.** Korean is subject–object–verb, and the verb always comes last. Roles are marked by particles attached to nouns rather than by position, which is why word order is flexible in a way English and Spanish word order is not.

- Topic 은/는 versus subject 이/가 — the hardest distinction at this level, and the one to start living with early
- Object 을/를
- Location and time 에, dynamic location 에서
- 도 (also), 와/과 and 하고 (and, with)
- 의 for possession, usually dropped in speech

**The copula and existence.**

- 이다 / 아니다 for "is" and "is not"
- 있다 / 없다 for existence and possession — one verb covers "there is", "I have" and "I am at"

**Present tense and speech level.** Korean verbs conjugate for politeness, not for person. This replaces the European habit of conjugating for I/you/he.

- The polite 해요 form: -아요 / -어요 / -해요, and how the stem's final vowel decides which
- The formal -(스)ㅂ니다 form, which you should recognise even if you speak in 해요
- 반말 exists; do not use it yet with anyone you have just met

**Past and future.**

- Past -았어요 / -었어요
- Near future and intention -(으)ㄹ 거예요
- The present tense also covers habitual actions, as in English

**Negation.**

- Short negation with 안 before the verb
- Long negation with -지 않다
- 못 for inability, which is a different idea from 안 and not interchangeable

**Numbers.** Korean has two complete number systems and you need both from the start.

- Sino-Korean 일, 이, 삼 for dates, money, minutes, phone numbers
- Native Korean 하나, 둘, 셋 for counting things, hours and age
- The common counters: 개, 명, 살, 시, 분, 권, 마리

**Sentence building.**

- Question words: 뭐, 누구, 어디, 언제, 어떻게, 왜, 얼마
- Questions need no word-order change — intonation and the ending do the work
- Joining clauses with -고 (and) and -아서/어서 (so, because)
- 주세요 for requests, which will carry you through every shop and restaurant

## Vocabulary areas

Aim for around 800 words you can retrieve, not merely recognise. Depth in these areas beats breadth.

- Greetings, introductions, and the fixed phrases Korean uses constantly (감사합니다, 죄송합니다, 괜찮아요)
- Family and relationships, including the fact that sibling words differ by the speaker's gender
- Numbers, dates, days, money, time
- Food, drink, restaurant and café language
- Shopping: prices, sizes, colours, 이거/그거/저거
- The city: 학교, 병원, 은행, 지하철, and asking where something is
- Daily routine verbs: 가다, 오다, 먹다, 자다, 공부하다, 일하다
- Weather and the seasons
- Adjectives for basic description and feeling

## What you should be able to do

Treat this as the exit test. Grammar you cannot deploy here is not yet learned.

- Read Hangul aloud at conversational speed, even if you do not know every word
- Introduce yourself: name, nationality, occupation, where you live
- Ask someone the same and understand the answer
- Order in a restaurant or café and ask what something costs
- Buy something, ask for a different size or colour, and understand the price spoken aloud
- Ask for and follow simple directions
- Talk about your family, your day and your hobbies in short sentences
- Say what you did yesterday and what you will do tomorrow
- Understand slow, clear speech on these familiar topics
- Write short simple sentences about yourself

## Common difficulties

**The verb arrives last.** In listening this is the real cost: you cannot know whether a sentence is positive, negative, a question or a request until it ends. Train yourself to hold the sentence and wait rather than translating as it goes.

**은/는 versus 이/가.** Expect this to stay uncomfortable for a long time — most learners are still refining it at TOPIK 4. Do not look for a rule that settles it. Start with the working approximation that 은/는 introduces or contrasts a topic and 이/가 identifies a subject, and let exposure do the rest.

**Two number systems.** The split is not arbitrary but it is not intuitive either. Learn each counter with the system it takes rather than learning the numbers separately.

**Speech levels have no European equivalent.** Choosing between 해요 and 반말 is not a register choice like *tú* and *usted*; it is closer to a decision about the relationship itself. When unsure, use 해요. Being slightly too polite is invisible; being too casual is not.

**Particles do not map onto prepositions.** 에 and 에서 both become "at" or "in" in English, and the difference is whether something exists there or something happens there. Learn them through examples, not through a translation.

**Subjects disappear.** Korean drops anything the context supplies. A sentence with no stated subject is normal, not incomplete.

## How to know you are ready for TOPIK 2

You are ready when Hangul is genuinely automatic, when the 해요 form comes out without assembling it, and when you can hold a slow conversation about yourself without rehearsing it first. Hesitating over verb endings means more time at this level, not a harder textbook.

TOPIK 1 is commonly estimated at somewhere around 100 to 150 hours of guided study for a European-language speaker — more than Spanish A1 would take, largely because of the writing system and the word order. Treat that as a rough scale, not a target.
